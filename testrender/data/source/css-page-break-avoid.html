The river bends twice before it reaches the mill, and in spring the water rises high enough to cover the lower path.

Walkers who know the valley take the upper track instead, past the orchard and the ruined chapel on the hill.

In the orchard the apple trees are older than the village records, planted by a family whose name nobody recalls.

The chapel lost its roof in a storm a century ago; swallows nest in the empty window frames every summer.

Beyond the chapel the path drops into a beech wood where the light turns green and the air smells of moss.

A wooden bridge crosses the stream at the bottom, its handrail worn smooth by generations of hands.

On the far side a meadow opens out, full of cowslips in April and grasshoppers in August.

The old miller used to say that the meadow flooded exactly once every seven years, never more and never less.

Nobody has checked his claim properly, although the schoolteacher keeps a notebook of the water levels.

From the meadow a lane leads back towards the square, lined with stone walls and blackberry hedges.

At the corner stands a bakery whose ovens have not gone cold, people say, since the day it opened.

Its bread is dense and dark, made with rye from the fields above the quarry and water from the spring.
