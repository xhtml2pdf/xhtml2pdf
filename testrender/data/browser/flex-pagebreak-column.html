Reading the river is the first thing a pilot learns, and the last thing he stops learning. The surface tells him where the bar has moved since spring.

A wind reef stands up straight and sharp; a bluff reef only shows as a faint dimple, and a new pilot will run over it without ever knowing it was there.

The boat draws less than nine feet, which sounds generous until the water drops a foot a day for a fortnight and the chutes begin to close one by one.

Every crossing has a name, and the names outlast the crossings: banks cave, points build out, and a bend that swallowed a season of freight is meadow now.

At night the shapes go first and the pilot steers by the memory of them, holding the wheel against a darkness he has to trust he has already seen.

Fog is worse than dark. In the dark a shore is a shade against the sky; in fog there is no sky, and the leadsman's voice is the only thing that moves.

The apprentice keeps a notebook until he learns that the notebook is useless, because by the time a page is full the river has gone and written another.

What he wants in the end is not knowledge of the river but an instinct about it, the sort that wakes him when the engines change note at three in the morning.

Coming up is harder than going down, because the boat is slow against the current and has all the time in the world to be set on a bar she cannot leave.

A pilot who has been off the river a single season comes back a passenger, and the humility of it is the only part of the trade nobody has to be taught.

The lead comes up marking twain and the cub relaxes, which is the moment the old man tells him that twain is a number and not a promise about the next length.

Low water is honest. It shows the bones of the channel and lets a careful man learn in a month what a flood would have hidden from him for a year.

By the end the river has stopped being beautiful to him and become a page of print, and he will tell you that the trade cost him the sunset it gave him.

Sounding out a crossing in a yawl at midnight is cold, wet, tedious work, and the cub does it because the alternative is to guess and to be wrong in front of everyone. The yawl goes ahead with a lantern and a pole, and the boat follows the light at a crawl while the leadsman calls the bottom back over the water. Nobody on board pretends to enjoy it, and nobody suggests skipping it either, because the one crossing you did not sound is the one that will hold you for a week. The pole finds the ridge, the lantern swings twice, and the wheel comes over a spoke at a time.

A steamboat's reputation rests on her pilot, and a pilot's rests on the season nobody remembers, when nothing whatever went wrong between Cairo and the Gulf.

The association fixed wages by refusing to let an unqualified man near a wheel, and the companies who swore they would break it were petitioning to join within a year.

When the railways came the river did not dry up; it simply stopped mattering, which for a trade built entirely on mattering turned out to be the same thing.
